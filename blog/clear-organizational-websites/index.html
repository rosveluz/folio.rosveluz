What an Organization's Website Needs to Communicate Clearly

A practical starting point for accounting offices, trade unions, and organizations: explain who you serve, make useful information accessible, and give visitors a clear next step.

3 min read

Start with the Visitor's Question

For an accounting office, trade union, or established organization, I would begin a website discussion with a simple question: what does someone need to understand or accomplish when they arrive?

The answer may be to check whether a service is relevant, find member information, understand the organization's role, or contact the right office. These are useful starting points for design because they connect the website to a real communication task.

A distinctive visual identity still matters. But I would judge the first screen by whether it introduces the organization clearly and offers sensible routes into the information, not by how much animation it contains.

Explain Who You Serve

An introductory statement should help someone recognize whether they are in the right place. What does the organization do, who is it for, and what is its scope?

For an accounting practice, that might mean naming the services and types of clients it works with. For a union, it might mean explaining membership and the support available. Those descriptions need to come from the organization, with any professional or eligibility claims checked before publication.

I would avoid asking a broad slogan to carry all of that meaning. A short, specific explanation can sit alongside the brand message and make it more useful.

Organize Around Useful Destinations

Before designing page layouts, I would list the main reasons people need the website. That list can become a starting point for navigation and content priorities.

  • Services or programs, with enough detail to understand their purpose.
  • Member or client resources, grouped by topic rather than upload date alone.
  • Organizational background and leadership.
  • News and publications, with clear dates and titles.
  • Contact details and the next step for different types of enquiry.

Not every organization needs every section. The important decision is what deserves a clear destination, rather than placing everything into a long homepage.

In my Eiger website design work, owner relations, company growth, and leadership have distinct treatments. They illustrate different communication tasks within related company websites, not a template that every organization should copy.

Make Documents Part of the Experience

Publications, forms, and policy documents should be understandable before someone downloads them. I would give each a descriptive title, a short explanation, and a visible date or version where relevant.

A document library also needs an owner. Someone should be responsible for replacing outdated files and checking whether a linked resource is still useful.

These decisions belong in the project brief. A clean layout cannot resolve uncertainty about which document is current or who should receive an enquiry.

Give People a Clear Next Step

Contact information should explain how to proceed. That can mean a general enquiry route, separate departmental contacts, or a specific application process. I would only show response times or service commitments that the organization can actually maintain.

On mobile, the same information needs to remain readable and reachable. Navigation, long headings, document links, and contact details all deserve review at narrow screen widths.

Define the Work Before the Look

My preferred starting brief would identify the audiences, their main questions, the content owners, and the pages needed to answer those questions. Visual design can then give that structure clarity and character.

For organizations considering a website project, that is a useful first conversation: not just what the site should look like, but what it needs to communicate.
